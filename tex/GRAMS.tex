\section{Liquid argon time projection chamber detector}
\label{chap:GRAMS:detectors}
The Liquid Argon Time Projection Chamber (LArTPC) is a powerful detection technology that has evolved significantly since its inception. The concept was first proposed by Nygren in 1974 as a method for precision tracking and calorimetry in particle physics experiments \cite{NYGREN201822}. Early implementations demonstrated the technology's ability to provide excellent spatial resolution and energy reconstruction capabilities through ionization charge collection in a liquid argon medium.

Over the decades, LArTPC technology has matured through numerous experiments, including the pioneering work at Fermilab and subsequent large-scale implementations. The technology gained prominence with experiments such as MicroBooNE, which validated LArTPC performance for neutrino detection \cite{MicroBooNE:2007ivj}. More recently, the Deep Underground Neutrino Experiment (DUNE) has adopted LArTPC as its primary detection technology for next-generation physics research. The key advantages of LArTPC detectors include exceptional spatial resolution (sub-millimeter scale), excellent calorimetric capabilities for energy measurement, and the ability to reconstruct complex particle interaction topologies \cite{FALCONE2022167217}. These characteristics make LArTPC technology particularly suitable for both astrophysical gamma-ray observations and rare event searches, as demonstrated by the GRAMS mission.

The LArTPC operates on the principle of ionization charge collection in liquid argon. When a charged particle traverses the detector volume, it ionizes argon atoms along its path, creating electron-ion pairs. An applied electric field drifts the free electrons toward the anode (charge readout plane), while the ions drift toward the cathode. The charge collected at the anode is proportional to the energy deposited by the particle, enabling precise energy measurement and calorimetry.

Scintillation light is produced during the ionization process and is detected by photomultiplier tubes (PMTs) or silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs) positioned at the cathode. This light signal provides the initial trigger and timing reference (T$_0$) for the event. The time difference between the scintillation light detection and the charge signal arrival determines the drift time, which directly translates to the vertical (Z) coordinate of the interaction via the known electron drift speed. Combined with the two-dimensional information from the charge readout grid, the full three-dimensional position of the event can be reconstructed with sub-millimeter precision.

The pixel-grid or mesh-style structure of the charge readout allows determination of the horizontal (X and Y) coordinates by identifying which readout channels recorded a signal; details are given in Section~\ref{chap:GRAMS:pGRAMS:RandD:detector}. This combination of spatial information from multiple detection mechanisms enables comprehensive event reconstruction and particle identification, making the LArTPC ideal for complex interaction topologies.

\subsection{GRAMS Detection Concept for MeV Gamma-rays}
\label{chap:GRAMS:concept:gamma}

As Fig.~\ref{fig:GRAMS_Concept} shows, particles entering the LArTPC detector excite and ionize argon atoms, producing scintillation light and ionization electrons. The scintillation light measured by \ac{SiPM}s is used for triggering and timing the event. The ionization electrons drift in an applied electric field and are collected at anode planes with a $\sim 2.8$~mm pitch of strips. The signals induced on the anode provide the $x$ and $y$ coordinates of the event, while the drift time of the ionization electrons gives the $z$ position. The ability to reconstruct three-dimensional space points without a multi-layered design is one of the key advantages of GRAMS compared to other gamma-ray detectors with semiconductors or scintillation crystals.

To reduce the coincident background during the finite collection time of the ionization signal, the LArTPC drift volume is segmented into ``cells'' with PTFE sheets. The detector can be further upgraded for a future satellite mission focused on gamma-ray observations. The overall instrumental size could reach $\sim 1.5$~m $\times 1.5$~m $\times 30$~cm, without the outer scintillator layer. The fine anode-tile pitch ($\sim 0.2$~mm) enables tracking of Compton-scattered electrons. A calorimeter could also be added around the detector, such as a \ac{CZT} detector similar to that proposed for the future satellite mission e-ASTROGAM~\cite{10.1007/s10686-017-9533-6}, in order to measure escaping gamma rays and pair-produced electrons and positrons. Development of the prototype detector is currently underway, and the details of the readout electronics and telemetry system will be finalized in future work.

The MeV gamma-ray survey requires accurate reconstruction of the photon energy and direction. For energies above the electron-positron pair-production threshold ($E > 2m_e c^2$), GRAMS uses the precise tracking capability of the LArTPC to reconstruct the momentum of the electron-positron pair to determine the incident gamma ray. At lower energies, where gamma rays preferably undergo Compton scattering and photo-absorption, GRAMS relies on accurately determining the position and energy of the Compton electron and the photo-absorption site.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{fig/GRAMS_Compton_rings.png}
    \caption{Compton-scattering events in GRAMS. Three or more Compton “Event Circles” provide the direction of the gamma-ray source \cite{aramaki2020dual}.}
    \label{fig:GRAMS_compton_ring}
\end{figure}

Reconstruction of the incident gamma-ray energy $E$ and cone angle $\theta$ can be estimated by the Compton equation \cite{KAMAE1987254,DOGAN1990501}. A gamma ray may undergo a number of Compton scatterings before being photo-absorbed or even escape the sensitive volume of the detector (see Fig.~\ref{fig:GRAMS_compton_ring}). For one or two Compton scatterings followed by photo-absorption, the energy and cone angle can be calculated as below:

\begin{align}
E &= E_1 + E_2 + E_3 \\
\cos\theta &= 1 - m_e c^2 \left( \frac{1}{E_2 + E_3} - \frac{1}{E_1 + E_2 + E_3} \right) \\
\cos\theta' &= 1 - m_e c^2 \left( \frac{1}{E_3} - \frac{1}{E_2 + E_3} \right)
\end{align}

where $m_e$ is the electron mass, $E_1$, $E_2$, and $E_3$ are the deposited energies by the Compton scatterings and photo-absorption, and $\theta$ and $\theta'$ are the first and the second Compton scattering angles. For a single Compton scattering followed by a photo-absorption, the above simplifies since there is no $\theta'$ and $E_2 = 0$.

For an event with three or more Compton scatterings, the angle and energy can be reconstructed as below:

\begin{align}
E &= E_1 + E_2 + E'_3 \\
\cos\theta &= 1 - m_e c^2 \left( \frac{1}{E_2 + E'_3} - \frac{1}{E_1 + E_2 + E'_3} \right) \\
E'_3 &= -\frac{E_2}{2} + \sqrt{ \frac{E_2^2}{4} + \frac{E_2 m_e c^2}{1 - \cos\theta'} }
\end{align}

where $E'_3$ is the energy of the gamma ray just after the second Compton scattering. The gamma ray after three or more Compton scatterings may escape from the detector. In each case, the cone angle $\theta$ gives a Compton ``Event Circle'' for each event, and the overlap of three or more ``Event Circles'' pinpoints the direction of the gamma-ray source (see Fig.~\ref{fig:GRAMS_compton_ring}). There has also been work to optimize the Compton-reconstruction efficiency \cite{TAKASHIMA2022166897, YONEDA2023102765}; however, because that topic is not a primary focus of this thesis, it is not discussed in detail here.

\subsection{GRAMS Detection Concept for Antimatter}
\label{chap:GRAMS:concept:antimatter}

The GRAMS antimatter survey involves capturing an antiparticle in a target material, with subsequent formation and decay of an exotic atom, similar to the \ac{GAPS} project mentioned in Section~\ref{chap:GAPS} \cite{Mori_2002,aramaki2013measurement,aramaki2016antideuteron}. The TOF plastic scintillators measure the velocity and angle of the incoming antiparticle. The antiparticle slows down and stops inside the LArTPC detector, where it forms an excited exotic atom with an argon atom. The exotic atom then emits atomic X-rays as it de-excites.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{fig/GRAMS_charged_particle_concept.png}
    \noindent\raggedright The GRAMS antimatter detection technique. The stopped antimatter forms an excited exotic atom that decays and emits atomic X-rays and annihilation products(pions and protons). The atomic X-rays, pion/proton multiplicities, and the stopping depth in the LArTPC provide the particle identification capability.
    \caption{GRAMS charged particle detection concept}
    \label{fig:GRAMS_charged_particle_concept}
\end{figure}

The energy of the atomic X-ray depends on the mass and atomic number of the antiparticle and the target atom. For an exotic atom formed with an antiproton and an argon atom, the two highest atomic X-ray energies are $58$~keV and $97$~keV. For an antideuteron with argon, these energies are $74$~keV and $114$~keV. At the end of the atomic cascade, the antiparticle subsequently annihilates in the nucleus with the emission of pions and protons. The number of pions and protons produced (pion/proton multiplicity) is roughly proportional to the number of antinucleons in the incoming antiparticle. This pion/proton multiplicity can also be used to identify the incoming antiparticle, see Fig.~\ref{fig:GRAMS_charged_particle_concept}.

Additionally, GRAMS can measure the stopping range of the incoming antiparticles with an excellent position resolution in the LArTPC. Fig.~9 shows the simulation results for the stopping range study, assuming the position resolution of $\sim 3$~mm in the LArTPC detector and the timing resolution of $\sim 0.4$~ns in the TOF system. Here, protons and deuterons were used instead of antiprotons and antideuterons to simplify the physics process. The solid black line represents the selection cut while the acceptances (probabilities for events in the right side of the selection cut) are $2.7 \times 10^{-6}$ for antiprotons and $0.74$ for antideuterons.

These particle identification techniques (atomic X-rays, pion/proton multiplicities, and stopping range) will allow us to distinguish incoming antideuterons from others. For example, the TOF system will select slow incoming particles, which allows us to identify and reject the cosmic ray protons since low-energy protons may not be able to produce relativistic pions as seen in the nuclear annihilation. GRAMS could also identify cosmic ray species based on the combination of the TOF, $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x$ energy deposit, and the stopping range, which can be used for the solar modulation study.

The detection concept and particle-identification techniques were validated and demonstrated in the accelerator test with an antiproton beam, as well as in the prototype flight for the GAPS experiment \cite{aramaki2013measurement,VONDOETINCHEM201493,MOGNET201424, FUKE20141432}. The expected mimic/background events could be as small as $0.01$ during the LDB flight.

\label{chap:GRAMS:eGRAMS}

An engineering balloon flight with a small-scale LArTPC (eGRAMS) was conducted on July 27, 2023, to establish a system for safely operating an LArTPC at balloon altitudes and to obtain cosmic-ray data from the LArTPC. The flight was launched from the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency’s (JAXA) Taiki Aerospace Research Field in Hokkaido, Japan. The total flight duration was 3 hours and 12 minutes, including a level flight of 44 minutes at a maximum altitude of 28.9 km. The flight system landed at sea and was successfully recovered.

\noindent\textbf{eGRAMS Flight summary, July 27, 2023:}

On July 26 2023, liquid argon (LAr) was loaded into the detector for a balloon flight the next day. The eGRAMS balloon launched on July 27 at 03:55 AM, followed a boomerang trajectory due to wind patterns, and reached a maximum altitude of 28.9 km. After a level flight, the gondola was released at 06:43 AM and landed safely on the sea at 07:07 AM, and was later recovered undamaged. Prior to detachment, the LAr was released from the vessel starting at 06:33 AM, with the process confirmed complete by 07:00 AM. Throughout the mission, the detector operated successfully: data was recorded continuously, the high voltage was carefully managed to prevent discharge, and trigger levels were adjusted to capture low-energy gamma-ray events. Internal pressure and temperature of the LAr vessel remained stable and within expected ranges, confirming the argon stayed liquid until evacuation began. The LAr release process experienced some pressure fluctuations due to the rapid solidification and vaporization of argon at low atmospheric pressure, but evacuation was completed as altitude decreased.

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:eGRAMS_setup} (top-left), the LAr vessel (shown inside red highlight) is placed in the center of the gondola with two ballast boxes on either side of it. \subsection{Charge Sensitive Preamp (CSP)}
\label{chap:GRAMS:eGRAMS:CSP}
A charge-sensitive preamplifier (CSP) converts charge collected from the detector into a pulse with a characteristic amplitude. This amplitude directly corresponds to the energy deposited in the detector volume. In addition, because of the pixel-grid structure of the tile, the corresponding X and Y coordinates of a given event can be determined by identifying which CSP channels responded. This information, combined with the Z coordinate derived from the time delay between the SiPM response and the CSP response, allows full event reconstruction.

Pins 3, 4, 5, and 6 provide +5 V power, GND, signal out, and -5 V power, respectively. \textbf{(c)} Designed \ac{PCB} layout.

    \label{fig:preamppicture}

The CSP for the LArTPC has been designed and constructed at Northeastern University, and a picture of a prototype version is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:preamppicture}. 

This is a 2-layer printed circuit board with two different stages of amplification circuitry. The first amplification stage involves integrating the incoming charge signal to produce an exponential decay voltage waveform, and the second stage amplifies the voltage signal further to achieve a good \ac{SN} ratio. Pins 1 and 2 connect to the TPC tile or an injection board for bench testing. Pins 3, 4, 5, and 6 provide +5 V power, GND, signal out, and -5 V power, respectively. For the first amplification stage, a dual \ac{JFET} connected in parallel is used to minimize the equivalent input noise charge. The $0.5~\text{pF}$ feedback capacitor and $1~\text{G}\Omega$ resistor are selected to provide an appropriate gain and decay time constant. The second stage uses a low-noise operational amplifier to further amplify the voltage signal. Critical components are selected based on their low-noise performance under cryogenic conditions; see \ac{BOM} Table~\ref{tab:CSP_BOM} for the full component list.

To study, optimize, and calibrate the \ac{CSP}, a bench test was established to quantify the injected charge and measure the output signal response. The setup involves a pulse generator that sends a square-wave signal to a voltage-divider circuit. The output of the voltage divider is connected to an injection capacitor of known capacitance: a $1~\text{pF}$ capacitor acts as an injection detector, while a $50~\text{pF}$ capacitor acts as a dummy detector. The value of the dummy-detector capacitance was varied to simulate the possible strip capacitance of the detector tile. At the output end, an analog shaper was used to shape the signal with different shaping times. Fig.~\ref{fig:CSP_testbench} shows the bench setup and the resulting signal \ac{FWHM} under different shaping times. Data were taken with different grounding configurations to identify the most stable operating condition for the \ac{CSP}s. With the charge conversion
\begin{align}\label{equ:Charge_calculation}
    Q&=\varepsilon_{Ar}\times N_e\\
    &=\varepsilon_{Ar}\times \frac{Q_{input}}{e}\\
    &=\frac{\varepsilon_{Ar}}{e}\times C_{inject}V_{input}\\
    &=\frac{25}{1.6\times10^{-19}}\times10^{-12}\times0.012~\text{V}\\
    &=1.875\times10^6~\text{eV}=1.875~\text{MeV}
\end{align}
where $\varepsilon_{Ar}=25~\text{eV}$ and the electron charge is $e=1.6\times10^{-19}~\text{C}$. $C_{inject}$ is the injection-capacitor value, $1~\text{pF}$. $V_{input}$ is the input voltage from the pulse generator, $12~\text{mV}$, after the voltage divider. With this input-charge relation, the readout voltage signal can be converted to an input-charge energy value. The conversion relation is shown in Eq.~\ref{equ:Charge_conversion}.
\begin{equation}\label{equ:Charge_conversion}
    1.875~\text{MeV}=7.5~\text{V}\Longrightarrow 1~\text{keV}=4~\text{mV}\Longrightarrow 1~\text{mV}=0.25~\text{keV}
\end{equation}
The \ac{CSP} was successfully operated at liquid argon temperature and achieved performance corresponding to an energy resolution as low as 18.92 keV, based on the conversion from Eq.~\ref{equ:Charge_conversion}.

As this \ac{CSP} design is adapted from the nEXO experiment, which has the readout electronics outside the cryogenic system \cite{Li_2019}, several modifications were made to optimize the design for the GRAMS application. The \ac{CSP} was redesigned to be compact enough to fit inside the GRAMS LArTPC cryostat, which has limited space. The power consumption was also reduced to minimize heat dissipation inside the cryostat because of the balloon mission power-budget constraint. Another major factor is that Texas Instruments has discontinued production of the BF862 \ac{JFET} that was previously used and tested. We tested several different \ac{JFET} candidates (BF862, MMBFJ310LT1G, 2SK932, 2SK3557, and CPH3910) and selected MMBFJ310LT1G and 2SK932 as the best-performing ones at cryogenic temperature. 

The eGRAMS mission used three \ac{CSP}s to validate operation during balloon flight, see Fig.~\ref{fig:eGRAMS_setup}, while the pGRAMS design will involve a total of 180 \ac{CSP}s to read out all active channels. They are divided into nine sections, each of which will have separate X- and Y-channel readouts. Power to the \ac{CSP}s will be provided through a dedicated power flange on the cryostat. This will branch out to individual power boards, which will distribute power to each connected \ac{CSP}. The \ac{CSP} output is driven outside the cryostat using a DB-62 connector through 110~$\Omega$ twisted-pair cables. It is then routed to the charge-signal shaper designed by Columbia University. 

Several considerations remain in the CSP design, including liquid argon purity and compatibility with low-temperature operation. The solder masks on fabricated CSPs to be used in pGRAMS will be removed because of purity considerations. Several op-amp candidates are also being tested for cryogenic compatibility.

The \ac{CSP} v5 was calibrated with a charge-injection board that makes use of a voltage divider, an injection capacitor of 1 pF, and a dummy capacitor used to mimic the pGRAMS detector’s assumed 50 pF capacitance. The tests demonstrated that the \ac{CSP} noise \ac{FWHM} can be as low as 20~keV at room temperature. As Fig.~\ref{fig:CSP_detector_capacitance_effect} shows, we changed the dummy detector capacitance to simulate possible detector-tile strip capacitance. The result shows that the noise performance of the \ac{CSP} is affected by the detector capacitance, which is expected. With lower detector capacitance, the noise performance is better, and compared with the pGRAMS detector strip capacitance of around 15 pF, we could achieve around 10 keV energy resolution. Under liquid argon cryogenic conditions, however, the injection capacitor value may vary, so the conversion relation from Eq.~\ref{equ:Charge_conversion} cannot be fully trusted at cryogenic temperature; this is still under investigation.

The T98 experiment was conducted at the K1.8BR beamline of the J-PARC Hadron Experimental Facility, which is capable of providing a high-purity antiproton beam with a maximum momentum of $1.1~\text{GeV}/c$ to observe high-statistics antiproton-capture events. The facility utilizes secondary particles produced by irradiating a $30~\text{GeV}$ proton beam onto an Au target. The K1.8BR beamline employs an \ac{ESS} to select the mass of the beam profile. The operating principle of the ESS is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:ESS_principle} (left). Particles bent by the upstream magnetic field pass through a region with an applied electric field, where their trajectory is reversed, and are subsequently bent again by a downstream magnet. Only particles with a given mass are transported downstream.

Multiple ToF counters, gas chambers, and \ac{AC} detectors are installed along the beamline. The combined use of \ac{TOF} and \ac{AC} enables high-purity selection of antiprotons and other species. Fig.~\ref{fig:ESS_principle} (right) shows the \ac{TOF} distribution for beams tuned to antiprotons (black) and deuterons (red), demonstrating effective separation of pions from antiprotons and deuterons.

\section{Prototype Flight pGRAMS}
\label{chap:GRAMS:pGRAMS}
pGRAMS is a precursor mission to the future GRAMS Science Mission, which will simultaneously target MeV gamma-ray observations and indirect dark matter searches through antimatter detection, and will serve as the first liquid argon Compton camera to be launched. It is funded by NASA's \ac{APRA} program and is led by Northeastern University, Columbia University, and \ac{GSFC}.

As the follow-on project to the 2023 GRAMS engineering flight (eGRAMS, Section~\ref{chap:GRAMS:eGRAMS}), pGRAMS builds on the knowledge gained there and advances the program in several directions. The pGRAMS payload is intended to verify the performance of a scaled GRAMS detector for MeV gamma-ray and cosmic charged-particle detection, validate the cryostat design and its thermal performance, demonstrate the combined particle-tracking capability of the time-of-flight (TOF) and multiplicity detector (MPD) systems, and test the flight firmware and software. These accomplishments from the pGRAMS flight will directly inform the future science flight, enabling exploration of both the MeV-gap region and the low-energy cosmic-antiparticle region ($\leq 0.2~\text{GeV}/n$), which is directly relevant to dark matter searches.

\noindent Specifically for pGRAMS, the goals are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item To demonstrate the flight capability of the prototype detector, including the MiniGRAMS liquid argon time projection chamber (\ac{LArTPC} size of $30~cm \times 30~cm \times 20~cm$) and the \ac{TOF}/\ac{MPD} systems.
    \item To demonstrate the capability to capture cosmic muons and reconstruct their tracks.
    \item To demonstrate the capability to identify Compton-scattering events.
    \item Although scientific observations are not the top priority of the pGRAMS flight, to observe some of the brightest gamma-ray sources.

\end{itemize}


\noindent To achieve our goals, there are also requirements for both our system and balloon flight as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The mass and power budgets are 500 kg and 250 W, respectively. The 500 kg mass budget could be relaxed by sacrificing flight altitude. The 250 W power budget could be increased by adding more batteries onboard, although that would also increase the total weight.
    \item The flight-altitude requirement is $\geq 25$ km, and the level-flight-time requirement is $\geq 6$ hours. Because this is a one-day-scale mission, no active cooling or purification system will be prepared.
    \item The gondola size constraint is $1.5~m \times 1.5~m \times 1.5~m$.

\end{itemize}

Based on these requirements, we designed the system shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:system_diagram}. The entire pGRAMS payload can be divided into the detector, \ac{DAQ}, communication, and power-distribution (\ac{PDU}) systems. The detector system contains the \ac{LArTPC} and the \ac{TOF}/\ac{MPD}. As the primary detector, the \ac{LArTPC} serves as the main instrument for MeV gamma-ray and cosmic charged-particle detection. The \ac{TOF}/\ac{MPD} acts as an auxiliary detector for particle identification and direction determination. The sealed enclosure near the main detector houses devices that require an environment incompatible with stratospheric conditions, mainly the \ac{DAQ} system. The communication system is handled by the hub computer, while the \ac{PDU} powers all subsystems. The upper-right part of the system diagram shows the flight avionics from the flight provider World View \cite{worldviewspace2025}, which handle flight control and telemetry transmission throughout the flight.

\subsubsection{microGRAMS Detector}
\label{chap:GRAMS:pGRAMS:RandD:detector}
As Fig.~\ref{fig:microGRAMS_RandD} shows, the microGRAMS detector is constructed with three major systems: a light readout cathode, a charge readout anode, and a drifting E-field shaping structure.

\noindent\textbf{Light readout cathode:} The cathode is made with a mesh structure to allow scintillation light to pass through and be detected by the \ac{SiPM} array installed at the bottom of the detector body. The cathode mesh is made of copper with $81\%$ optical transparency to allow efficient light collection. The \ac{SiPM} array consists of 8 visible SiPMs and 8 VUV SiPMs, each with an active area of $6~mm \times 6~mm$. These SiPMs are arranged in a $4 \times 4$ grid pattern, covering a total active area of $24~mm \times 24~mm$. Each group of 4 SiPMs is biased and read out, allowing for precise timing and position information for the scintillation light signals. The SiPMs are mounted on a custom-designed PCB that provides mechanical support and electrical amplification. This SiPM readout is compatible with one cell out of the 9 cells in the science flight detector design.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{fig/pGRAMS_tile.png}
    \caption{A. pGRAMS tile design demonstration. B. pGRAMS tile front-side detail. C. pGRAMS tile back-side detail. See more details in Section~\ref{chap:GRAMS:pGRAMS:detector}. D. microGRAMS tile photograph.}
    \label{fig:tilecloseup}
\end{figure}

\noindent\textbf{Charge readout anode:} The anode is designed with a strip readout structure to provide two-dimensional position information for the ionization charge signals. The anode consists of 32 strips in both the X and Y directions, with each strip having a width of $2.8~mm$ and a separation of $200~\mu m$. This configuration allows for a total active area of $96~mm \times 96~mm$. Each strip is connected to a \ac{CSP} for signal amplification and processing, as mentioned in Section~\ref{chap:GRAMS:eGRAMS:CSP}. A demonstration of the microGRAMS tile is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tilecloseup}\textbf{D}. At the same time, panels A, B, and C show design demonstrations of the pGRAMS tile, which shares the same design concept at a larger size. See more details in Section~\ref{chap:GRAMS:pGRAMS:detector}.

\noindent\textbf{Light readout cathode:} pGRAMS will make use of \ac{SiPM}s that are sensitive to light in the visible and ultraviolet regimes. The SiPM sensors selected for the flight have been manufactured by Hamamatsu Photonics and procured by Northeastern University. Half of the SiPMs comprising the detector are the Hamamatsu S13360-6075CS, which are sensitive to light between 270 and 900 nm. The remaining SiPMs are the Hamamatsu S13370-6075CN, which are sensitive to a larger portion of the electromagnetic spectrum, from 120 to 900 nm. All SiPMs use the same PCB-mount package and have a sensitive area of $6 \times 6~mm$. Both models are also constructed with a microcell pitch of 75~um, which offers the highest \ac{PDE} for this class of SiPM. The S13360-6075CS and S13370-6075CN have PDEs of about 50\% and 35\%, respectively, at about 480 nm.
Since most light produced by liquid argon scintillation falls in the ultraviolet to vacuum-ultraviolet range, \ac{TPB} is used as a wavelength shifter to shift the scintillation light into the visible range. Adhered to the interior walls of the TPC cells are Mylar sheets that have been coated with a thin ($\sim$1 $\mu$m) layer of \ac{TPB} using a vacuum evaporation process developed at Waseda University, Tokyo \cite{Aoyama_2020}. In this configuration, isotropically emitted scintillation light is converted into visible blue light (430 nm) and re-emitted isotropically by the TPC walls. Further studies are planned to investigate the feasibility of evaporatively coating TPB directly onto the SiPM sensors. The Yorita Lab from the Waseda University / University of Tokyo group has extensive experience with this technique \cite{yoritakohei2025}.
For each TPC cell, 16 SiPMs are ganged in a hybrid configuration. Specifically, 4 SiPMs are connected together, resulting in a $2\times2$ quadrant configuration for each TPC cell. Each quadrant (4 SiPMs) is read out as a single channel.
Using the hybrid ganging scheme, multiple SiPMs can be read out as a single channel while keeping the capacitive load on the input of the preamplifier to a minimum. This scheme also allows the parallel biasing of multiple SiPMs, which only requires the bias voltage that would be used for a single SiPM. The bypass capacitors used in the hybrid ganging scheme also reduce the fall time of the signal pulse compared to a group of SiPMs ganged in a purely parallel setup.

\noindent\textbf{E-field shaping structure:} The drifting electric field is established using a series of field shaping rings made of copper embedded in FR4 PCB boards. These rings are evenly spaced at $5~mm$ along the height of the detector, ensuring a uniform electric field throughout the active volume. The field shaping rings are connected to a high-voltage power supply through a resistor chain, allowing for precise control of the electric field strength. Also, as mentioned in the above anode and cathode sections, the copper mesh behaves as an extra field-shaping electrode to ensure a uniform E-field near the edges, see Fig.~\ref{fig:microGRAMS_RandD}(b) and (d). For the anode mesh, aside from the field-shaping purpose, it also acts as a signal shaper to block the induced signal and make the rising peak of the raw charge signal more prominent, see the comparison without and with the anode-mesh effect on the charge readout in Fig.~\ref{fig:event_comparison}.

For the microGRAMS TPC we tested in the lab, we performed an E-field simulation with FEMTET software \cite{muratasoftware} to optimize the field uniformity. The simulation result is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:Efield_simulation}. The entire active volume has a very uniform E-field distribution except for the edge region close to the field-shaping rings. We will use a fiducial-volume cut to avoid the edge effect during data analysis.
Another important parameter is the anode-mesh blocking efficiency. To address this problem, we separated the TPC volume vertically into two parts: one for the entire TPC body with a vertical distance $d_2$ and total resistance $R_2$, and one for the anode-mesh region with a vertical distance $d_1$ and total resistance $R_1$. We then calculated the E-field distribution theoretically along the center line of the TPC volume based on a fixed external high voltage $V$.

\begin{align}
    V_1 + V_2 &= V\\
    \frac{V_1}{R_1} &= \frac{V_2}{R_2}
\end{align}
Then we could resolve the E-field value in each region as:
\begin{align}
    E_1 &= \frac{V_1}{d_1} = \frac{R_1}{R_1+R_2}\frac{V}{d_1}\\
    E_2 &= \frac{V_2}{d_2} = \frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}\frac{V}{d_2}
\end{align}
Here, based on previous work, the ratio of the E-field between these two regions is desired to be $E_2/E_1 = 10$. Based on the above equations, we could derive the resistance-ratio requirement for our detector setup. This ratio is also applied to the design of the pGRAMS detector, see Section~\ref{chap:GRAMS:pGRAMS:detector}.

\subsubsection{\ac{LAr} Purity and Electron Lifetime}
\label{chap:GRAMS:pGRAMS:RandD:purity}
To achieve optimal performance of the liquid argon time projection chamber (LArTPC), it is crucial to maintain high purity levels of the liquid argon (LAr) used in the detector. Impurities such as oxygen, water, and nitrogen can significantly affect the electron lifetime and light attenuation, which are key parameters for accurate charge-signal detection. Extensive research has been done on this topic in the past to better understand electron drift in argon \cite{CARUGNO1990580, BETTINI1991177, ZHANG2021165491}. From these studies, we know that the main impurity molecules that affect the electron lifetime are oxygen and water. Even at the \ac{ppm} level, these impurities can have a significant impact on the performance of the LArTPC.

To control these impurity molecules to the \ac{ppb} level, we implemented a purification system to purify the liquid argon that we bought from a vendor who claimed it was 99.999\% pure. We baked a filter that consists of 820 g of copper oxide (CuO) and 400 g of molecular sieve in a sealed stainless steel canister, see Fig.~\ref{fig:LAr_filter}. For preparation of the filter, we used flowing argon gas purging to accomplish dehydration, and a flowing argon-plus-hydrogen gas mixture to perform copper redox. The detailed procedure for filter regeneration will be published in future work.

After the filter was prepared, we developed a portable stand that can be used to fill liquid argon into the microGRAMS chamber. The entire filling-stand design is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:LAr_filling_setup}.

With the setup shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:LAr_filling_setup} top right and bottom, the operation procedure to fill liquid argon into the microGRAMS chamber is as follows:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
    \item With Edwards scroll pump connected to the LAr filter pump line, start the pump and evacuate the first region up to V1. This includes the MKS pressure gauge, so a drop in pressure should be observed.
    
    \item After letting the Edwards pump for $\sim 30$ seconds, open V1 and V2 to evacuate all lines to and from the filter. At this point, V3 and V4 will remain closed to isolate the filter.
    
    \item Open LAr dewar liquid valve a small amount to start flowing GAr into the filter lines while keeping the Edwards pumping on all lines. Once Edwards audibly starts working on the GAr, the Edwards valve V2 can slowly be closed. Once the Edwards is closed off, adjust the dewar valve to target a rate of $\sim 20$ Torr/second as read out on the MKS gauge.
    
    \item With the dewar valve set properly, open the Edwards valve V1 and let it evacuate the system again. While leaving the dewar valve alone, slowly throttle the Edwards valve V1 and bring the pressure up to $\sim 850$ Torr at the target rate of $\sim 20$ Torr/sec. Once the pressure reaches $\sim 750$ Torr, start closing V2 so that it is fully closed once the pressure reaches target of $\sim 850$ Torr.
    
    \item Turn off Edwards scroll pump.
    
    \item Make sure relief valve knob on UPS chamber relief line passive pressure valve is at its lowest setpoint (typically this turns out to be $\sim 820$ Torr).
    
    \item Have one person close the bellows valve to the turbo (V9) and have another person immediately open the fill valve (V6) to the chamber once the turbo is isolated.
    
    \item Modulate the fill valve (V6) to gradually bring pressure in the UPS chamber up towards $\sim 850$ Torr via the bypass line.
    
    \item Modulate the relief valve knob so that the equilibrium pressure without outward flow reaches $\sim 900$ Torr.
    
    \item Close the fill line valve V6 and then immediately close the bypass line V5 on the filter. At this point, the pressure in UPS will drop until the relief check valve closes. Then the UPS will be sealed at positive pressure and should stay relatively stable.
    
    \item Now open the top LAr filter valve (V3) and let pressure equalize between inside of filter and LAr dewar.
    
    \item Next, open the bottom LAr filter valve V4, and let pressure equalize again, similar to previous step. At this point the UPS chamber filling will be very similar to a direct fill.
    
    \item Make sure one person is ready at the dewar valve V0 and another person operating the fill line valve V6 and the main exhaust valve V7.
    
    \item LAr fill can now start. Slowly open the fill line valve V6 and flow GAr into the UPS chamber. Allow pressure to build until the passive relief check valve opens. Once the relief valve opens itself, adjust the setpoint to around $\sim 950$ Torr.
    
    \item Now the main exhaust V7 can be opened and the iteration between main exhaust and filling line can start.
    
    \item Open the main exhaust valve V7 until pressure drops to around $\sim 900$ Torr. At this point, gently increase the flow into the chamber via the fill line valve V6 and bring the pressure close to, but not higher than $\sim 1200$ Torr.
    
    \item Repeat last step, until the main exhaust is fully open.
    
    \item Monitor the pressure, and use the fill line valve V6 only to maintain pressure around $\sim 1500$ Torr. The chamber is now being cooled by GAr.
    
    \item Once the chamber is cold enough to allow LAr to condense, reduce the flow rate by adjusting fill valve V6 so that the equilibrium pressure is maintained around $\sim 900$ Torr. The chamber is now in slow LAr filling mode.
    
    \item Maintain conditions until the desired LAr level is achieved.
    
    \item Once desired LAr level is reached, close the main exhaust anywhere between 90\%--100\%. Make sure the relief valve is still able to relieve and maintain pressure.
    
    \item Once the pressure stabilizes, close the main exhaust and the fill line completely. The chamber is now ready for detector operation and data taking.
    
    \item Keep the filter open for 1--2 hrs and allow it to warm up while exposed to the LAr bath in the dewar. This ensures that no LAr is left inside the filter before it is sealed.
    
    \item Once the LAr filter is warmed up sufficiently (minimal frost visible on the lines and relatively warm to the touch), the bottom V4 and top valve V5 on the filter can be closed respectively.
    
    \item To finish the procedure, close the dewar LAr valve V0.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{CAEN Commercial \ac{DAQ}}
\label{chap:GRAMS:pGRAMS:RandD:CAEN}
For laboratory R\&D purposes, a fast and easily accessible \ac{DAQ} is needed to provide rapid digitization and data analysis. Because this system is not intended for balloon flight, low power consumption and minimal thermal dissipation are less restrictive design requirements. High-performance electronics that provide the desired sampling frequency are therefore preferable. The high-performance digitization cards V2740B and Vx2730 were purchased from CAEN.

The Vx2730 is ideal for waveform digitization and multi-channel analysis, particularly for signals from liquid scintillators coupled with SiPMs. 
Power to the board is provided by the VME64-X crate. The Vx2730 board requires a power input of +12~V, amounting to 6~W. This versatile digitizer supports charge-to-digital conversion firmware with per-channel configurable settings. It features a 14-bit resolution at a 500 MS/s sampling rate. The device supports 32 single-ended analog inputs via MCX connectors, offering a 4Vpp input range with software-selectable analog gain. Open FPGA programming is available through the SCI-Compiler graphical tool, providing customization and flexibility. It features fully programmable front panel I/Os, including 4 LEMO TTL/NIM and 16 LVDS connections. The on-board Zynq UltraScale+ FPGA, with an embedded Linux-based ARM processor, enables real-time processing to extract physical parameters from waveforms, ideal for high trigger rate applications. The board includes 5GB of total acquisition memory (DDR4), allowing it to save both raw waveforms and processed parameters.

There are 144 SiPMs installed in the pGRAMS detector, and a common SiPM bias HV is supplied to all of them. After pre-amplification, the 32 independent analog SiPM signals are driven outside the cryostat and digitized using a CAEN Vx2730 digitizer board, which can accommodate 32 channels simultaneously at 500 MHz with a 14-bit ADC. The Vx2730 board FPGA can be configured with customizable trigger logic to generate SiPM triggers based on multiple channel coincidences and summed pulse height. These signals are then processed by the NIM module to produce a single trigger pulse which is then distributed to the charge readout system and TOF system via LEMO as a negative NIM pulse of 100 ns width to initiate data readout. The Vx2730 will be configured to read out data in association with a light trigger, capturing the timestamp, waveform ID, uncalibrated energy ADC value, and the waveform itself over a $2~\mu$s duration surrounding the trigger time. The output data is sent to PC storage via USB or gigabit ethernet. Communication interfaces include USB 3.0 type-C and 1/10 Gigabit Ethernet, offering versatile options for data transfer and integration into various systems. The maximum data writing rate is 280 MB/s. For software development, the Vx2730 offers comprehensive support. Custom DAQ software can be developed using the CAEN-provided SDK for the embedded Linux processor and host PC, allowing for console-based operations. Additionally, the digitizer board fully supports CoMPASS and WaveDump2 readout software. Light data will be time-synchronized with the charge and time-of-flight data offline, using time markers synchronously timestamped in each readout system.

\subsection{pGRAMS Flight Detector}
\label{chap:GRAMS:pGRAMS:detector}
pGRAMS will make use of the ``MiniGRAMS'' LArTPC detector, which comprises a total of nine (9) optically isolated TPC cells, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:minigrams}, housed inside the cryostat with a custom flange. The LArTPC is constructed with
\begin{itemize}
    \item a top electron readout system with 180 charge-sensitive preamplifiers (CSPs) connected to a 2D charge-sensing tile to provide the electron-drift x-y geometry information, which is powered by a series of power boards to distribute power and signals at the same time.
    \item a series of field-shaping rings embedded in four side PCB boards to provide the drift E-field.
    \item an anode mesh and cathode mesh for field shaping and extracting drifted electrons.
    \item bottom 3$\times$3 SiPM cells to read out the signal-trigger light produced inside the active volume.
\end{itemize}

See Fig.~\ref{fig:minigrams}. The left figure shows a miniGRAMS TPC design demonstration. The top anode is constructed with a $330~mm \times 330~mm$ tile and 12 groups of \ac{CSP}s. The bottom cathode is constructed of 3 SiPM preamplifier boards, each of which holds 3 cells. Inside the TPC there are PTFE sheets to separate each cell. The right figure shows the miniGRAMS TPC schematic. Each field-cage ring for the TPC body is connected with a 100~M$\Omega$ resistor, while the anode-mesh region is connected with a 400~M$\Omega$ resistor to provide the desired E-field ratio between these two regions.

An E-field simulation was also performed for the miniGRAMS TPC design to verify that the field is sufficiently uniform for charge drift. The simulation was carried out with FEMTET software \cite{muratasoftware}, and the result is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:E-field}. The left plot shows the E-field vector cross section, the middle one shows the potential cross section, and the right plot shows the numerical increment along the central drift line. The E-field is uniform in the drift region, and there is a sharp increase in the E-field near the anode region, which is expected to help extract electrons from the drift region to the anode tile. Overall, the simulation indicates good charge-drift performance.

\noindent\textbf{Charge readout anode:} The charge-collection tile has a geometry of 33~cm$\times$33~cm, which shares the same design concept as described in Section~\ref{chap:GRAMS:pGRAMS:RandD:detector}. Its active area is 30~cm$\times$30~cm within the outer contour, while there will be no active area on the edges of each cell due to the separation. The tile itself comprises 96 strips in each direction (X and Y), spaced 2.6~mm apart and read out independently. Due to the cell separator, 90 strips in each direction will be active, which adds up to a total of 180 charge-readout channels. Each strip is made of pixels connected along the given direction and interplexed as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:microGRAMS_RandD}(a), (b), and (c). Once the charge reaches the tile, it induces signals on both X and Y strips, thereby determining the hit location. \subsection{Data acquisition system (DAQ)}
\label{chap:GRAMS:pGRAMS:DAQ}
As shown in the pGRAMS system diagram in Fig.~\ref{fig:system_diagram}, the \ac{DAQ} system is responsible for reading out the charge signals from the \ac{LArTPC} detector, digitizing them, and transmitting the data to the onboard computer for storage and analysis. The \ac{DAQ} system is designed to handle the high data rates generated by the TPC while also being robust enough to operate under the harsh conditions of a balloon flight. After passing through the cryostat and vacuum-jacket flanges, the amplified charge signals from the CSPs are fed into a special shaper board designed by Columbia University to further sharpen the signal rise for improved timing resolution. The shaped charge signals are then sent to the Nevis DAQ crate, which is housed inside the sealed enclosure, where they are digitized and transmitted to the DAQ computer via PCIe. At the same time, the TPC light signals are sent to a shaper card in the same Nevis crate for further shaping and digitization. The light trigger is also digitized and sent to the DAQ computer via PCIe, and it provides a trigger signal to the \ac{TOF} system for subsequent event merging.

\noindent\textbf{Charge DAQ Nevis DAQ crate Mechanical assembly:} The system comprises a 9U crate with a custom backplane, a crate controller, a clock card, a transmitter module, a trigger board, and three ADC+FEM (Front End Module) cards responsible for digitization and data processing. The crate is housed inside the sealed enclosure because of the environmental and thermal requirements.

\noindent\textbf{Charge DAQ digitization and Data Handling:} The analog TPC data are received by each ADC+FEM card in groups of 64 channels. The ADC part of the card was designed by Brookhaven National Lab, while the digital data handling part was designed by Columbia University, Nevis Labs. Within each card, the analog signals are digitized within eight AD9222 octal-channel, 12-bit ADC chips from Analog Devices, Inc., providing sufficient dynamic range to accommodate both gamma-ray and antiparticle detection, with a power consumption of 100~mW/channel. Signals are digitized at 16~MHz, but only one in eight samples are kept in the digital handling part of the board, reducing the sampling rate to effectively 2~MHz. The 2~MHz sampling rate is optimized by taking into consideration the expected pulse shape provided by the convolution of the front-end electronics, the expected electric field response, and the O(1)~$\mu$s diffusion effects which govern charge drift within the liquid argon. Digital handling is provided by the FEM part of the card, which houses a Stratix III Altera FPGA. Data from all 64 wires are first rearranged from channel order to time order, and duplicated into two readout streams---one that supports continuous readout after loss compression, and another that allows for lossless compression and readout upon receiving an external trigger. Only the latter option was chosen for pGRAMS.

\noindent\textbf{Charge DAQ readout Configuration and Data Flow:} The crate controller provides system configuration and slow I/O communication with each board in the readout crate. The system is run on an independent, 16~MHz clock, distributed synchronously to all electronics cards via the backplane. The clock resets upon a software-initiated run-start and is tracked in terms of a configurable (nominally set to 100~us) frame size and a 2~MHz sample number keeping track of time within the frame. All ADC+FEM cards are synchronized to this common 16~MHz clock. The trigger board is responsible for receiving and issuing trigger signals, as well as distributing accepted triggers to the crate controller. The crate controller subsequently propagates them to the transmitter module, which initiates sequential readout of each ADC+FEM card beginning with a configurable readout window prior to the trigger time, $\Delta t$, and extending forward in time spanning a total of 3$\Delta t$. The configurable size of $\Delta t$ is governed by the maximum drift time and spans three or four frames. To reduce the data volume, the FPGA trims the three or four frames to span the exact readout window required, 100~$\mu$s before the trigger plus 200~$\mu$s after the trigger. Optionally, lossless compression (Huffman) can be applied, and, after processing by the FPGA, the data passes to the crate backplane dataway. A token-passing scheme is utilized to transfer data from each FEM board to the data transmitter module in a controlled way, whereby each FEM, in the order of closest to furthest away from the XMIT module, receives a token, transmits its data to the XMIT, and passes the token on to the next FEM in the sequence. Each FEM sends all data associated with a particular trigger number. This data transfer is relayed via the otherwise passive crate backplane, and is limited to 512~MB/s. In the transmitter module, the data is buffered temporarily and sent to the DAQ machine via PCIe.

\noindent\textbf{Light DAQ capture light trigger:} The Trigger Board has the ability to receive, via the crate controller, DAQ-issued calibration triggers (via software), which are used explicitly for electronics and light calibration. The various input triggers can be independently pre-scaled, masked, and mixed together (OR or AND) to generate an event trigger, ensuring full coverage of the \ac{ROI}.

\noindent\textbf{Light DAQ Mechanical Assembly:} The nominal option for the pGRAMS SiPM readout makes use of custom readout electronics designed originally for the MicroBooNE LArTPC experiment, and is a complement to the charge readout system. Specifically, the light readout system can be housed together with the charge readout system in the same custom crate with a backplane distributing power and clock signals to a number of electronics boards:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Crate Controller: responsible for configuration and control at run start, status readback and GPS timing readback. Also receives trigger from trigger board and issues readout command.
    \item Shaper board: Receives raw analog signals from SiPMs. Shapes signals with a ~60ns rise time. Shaped signals are driven differentially to the ADC+FEM via front panel cables.
    \item ADC+FEM board: Performs digitization and data packaging. The board supports up to 64 channels (only 1 will be needed for pGRAMS).
    \item Transmitter board (XMIT): Responsible for shipping the ADC+FEM data. The XMIT hardware supports two possible streams: triggered, or continuous.
    \item Trigger board: Receives SiPM triggers from the ADC+FEM board. The trigger board issues triggers via ethernet connection to the crate controller.
    \item Clock module: 16MHz precision clock responsible for providing timing through the backplane to all boards.
\end{enumerate}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{fig/ADC_FEM_SCHEM.png}
    \caption{NEVIS DAQ schematic}
    \label{fig:DAQ_schematic}
\end{figure}

The SiPM ADC+FEM and three shaper boards can sit in the same crate as the charge readout system described earlier, in which case no extra crate, controller, \ac{XMIT}, clock, or trigger modules would be necessary. The Columbia light readout system therefore only requires 4 additional boards, in the form of 3 analog shaper boards (5V) and a single ADC+FEM board (12V/5V).

\noindent\textbf{Light DAQ Digitization and Data Handling:} The analog waveforms enter the SiPM shaper board via LEMO inputs and are 50-Ohm terminated at the shaper. The shaped signals (unipolar shaping, 60ns shaping time) are sent to the ADC+FEM board differentially via short front panel cables, and are digitized by the ADC at 64 MHz. The baseline of the receiver is set at ADC mid‐range, 2048. After digitization, the digitized raw pulse is delayed by d=4 64 MHz samples and subtracted from itself, giving DIFF(i) = rawpulse(i)‐rawpulse(id), where d is a parameter configurable by the run control. This sharpens the pulse by removing any low frequency noise and baseline shifts. The value of d depends on the signal shaping time, and can be adjusted in order to maximize signal to noise ratio. If rawpulse(i)‐rawpulse(i‐d) is negative, DIFF(i) is set to zero. The DIFF pulse (which is NOT recorded but only used for generating readout conditions and triggering) is discriminated to determine whether rawpulse(i) should be read out, as well as to form a trigger. For any enabled channel, there are two discriminators, Discr 0 and Discr 1. Each has an independently set threshold value. A two‐level discrimination is used. Discr 0 is the first level of discrimination and is a precondition for Discr 1 firing. Discr 0 has a very low threshold and is used to determine the timing of a pulse, thus avoiding slewing. A second level of discrimination is applied by Discr 1. Discr 1 typically has higher thresholds and is used to determine the actual readout of data and define timing windows for possible SiPM trigger generation. If the discriminators fire on a particular channel, a configurable number of 64MHz samples for that particular channel associated with the fired discriminators will be stored in the FEM channel FIFO. If a trigger, issued by the trigger board, intercepts the FEM delay memory, the SRAM discriminated data over four clock frames surrounding the trigger time will be read out. Assuming the nominal choice of 100 microseconds per clock frame, this means 400 microseconds of SiPM data for every triggered event. 

\textbf{Light DAQ Readout Configuration and Data Flow:} The overall readout system's internal communication and data flow is described earlier in the charge DAQ part. The transmitter module initiates controlled and sequential readout of all ADC+FEM cards in the crate. The SiPM ADC+FEM stores four clock frames' worth of data for each event, including the frame in which the trigger was received, one frame before it, and two frames after it. A token-passing scheme is utilized to transfer data from each FEM board to the data transmitter module in a controlled way, whereby each FEM, in the order of closest to furthest away from the XMIT module, receives a token, transmits its data to the XMIT, and passes the token on to the next FEM in the sequence. It is necessary for the SiPM ADC+FEM to sit farther from the XMIT module than the TPC ADC+FEMs.


\subsection{Cryostat and plumbing}
\label{chap:GRAMS:pGRAMS:cryostats}
As the working medium for the \ac{LArTPC} is liquid argon, a cryogenic system is required to provide cooling and maintain the liquid state. For the pGRAMS flight, a custom cryostat with a vacuum jacket was designed to provide thermal insulation. The inner vessel is made of aluminum and houses the miniGRAMS \ac{LArTPC} detector and the \ac{LAr}. The outer aluminum vessel provides a vacuum-insulation space between the two vessels to minimize convective heat transfer; multiple layers of insulation sheets further reduce radiative heat transfer. For flight, the cryostat flange is oriented downward to minimize the material above the active volume. The design is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pGRAMS_cryostat}. With this cryostat design, the \ac{LAr} state can be maintained for more than 24 hours without additional cooling, which is sufficient for the pGRAMS flight expected to last less than one day.
Detailed cryostat design files are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:designs}.

\subsection{Flight Computer System}
\label{chap:GRAMS:pGRAMS:computer}
The flight computer system is constrained by stratospheric compatibility, low power consumption, and system performance. Some selection requirements are listed as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The computer that sends telemetry data and controls the \ac{PDU} has to be stratosphere compatible, with a wide temperature range from $-60^\circ\text{C}$ to $+50^\circ\text{C}$.
    \item The computer that reads and writes \ac{DAQ} system data has to have enough storage space and high data-writing speed to avoid data loss. At the hardware level, it also needs two \ac{PCIe} slots as data-transfer ports, as mentioned in Section~\ref{chap:GRAMS:pGRAMS:DAQ}.
    \item The power consumption of the computer has to be as low as possible while still performing the necessary tasks.
    \item The operating system has to be Linux-based to be compatible with the \ac{DAQ} software and other devices we are developing.
    \item The extension of I/O ports has to be flexible enough to connect with various devices, including USB, RS232, SPI, etc.
\end{itemize}

After evaluating multiple options, we selected two computers for the pGRAMS flight computer system. One is the hub computer (Versalogic DS-EPM-31 BayCat) \cite{versalogic_baycat}, which is responsible for overall system control and telemetry data handling. The other is the DAQ computer (Versalogic VL-EPMe-51 Sabertooth) \cite{versalogic_sabertooth}, which is responsible for receiving data from the Nevis DAQ crate via PCIe and storing it on a local SSD. Both computers are running Ubuntu 22.04.
As shown in the pGRAMS system diagram in Fig.~\ref{fig:system_diagram}, the hub computer is mainly in charge of communication and control, while the DAQ computer is mainly in charge of data receiving and storage, as described below in Section~\ref{chap:GRAMS:pGRAMS:computer:hub} and Section~\ref{chap:GRAMS:pGRAMS:computer:daq}.

For the control system, pGRAMS decided to use the same framework that is being used and tested in the engineer flight \cite{Nakajima:2024fgx}. See software development at \cite{pGramsFC_2024}.

\subsubsection{Hub Computer}
\label{chap:GRAMS:pGRAMS:computer:hub}
Within the two-computer flight architecture, the hub computer is primarily responsible for communication and control. It handles communication with the ground station via Iridium and Starlink. All \ac{HK} data collected from various sensors are packaged and sent periodically to the ground station. At the same time, it receives commands from the ground station and distributes them to the corresponding devices, including the \ac{PDU}, the DAQ computer, and the \ac{TOF} system. This arrangement provides greater flexibility in the choice of hub computer because it does not need to handle the high data rate from the \ac{DAQ} system. We selected the Versalogic DS-EPM-31 BayCat \cite{versalogic_baycat} as the hub computer; this system is also used in another balloon experiment, ComPair \cite{10.1117/12.2628811}.
As shown in the pGRAMS system diagram in Fig.~\ref{fig:system_diagram} and Table~\ref{tab:hub_io_summary}, the hub computer will take one Ethernet connection from the flight provider World View's avionics \cite{worldviewspace2025} as the primary control interface. The second Ethernet connection will be used to connect with the DAQ computer for command and status exchange. The default USB ports are extended to six: the \ac{HK} board, 2$\times$ 4-wire \ac{RTD} readout modules, 2$\times$ \ac{TOF} bias-control board converters, and 1 spare. The communication between the hub computer and the \ac{PDU} will be through an SPI interface and a pair of power cables.

\subsection{Power Distribution Unit (PDU)}
\label{chap:GRAMS:PDU}
The power distribution unit (PDU) is responsible for distributing power from the battery packs to the various subsystems of the pGRAMS payload, including the flight computer system, \ac{DAQ} system, \ac{TOF} system, cryostat heaters, and other electronics. The PDU is designed to handle the high power requirements of the payload while remaining sufficiently robust for operation under balloon-flight conditions. It provides device-level power control, while the hub computer remains continuously powered because it serves as the control endpoint of the \ac{PDU}. The detailed design is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:design_PDU}.

As for the power source, we selected the SR182175 battery stack \cite{nasa_csbf_sr182175}, which has been widely used in balloon experiments. Each battery stack has a nominal voltage of 26 V (22 V - 30 V depending on the load) and a capacity of 50 Ah. With our power budget mentioned in Table~\ref{tab:pGRAMS_powerbudget}, we can estimate the mission time based on the number of battery stacks. With a total nominal load of 293.8375 W and a \ac{PDU} efficiency of 85\%, we can estimate the current and flight time for different numbers of battery stacks. Table~\ref{tab:pGRAMS_flight_time} shows the estimated flight times for different scenarios. According to the battery stack manual, it can perform as poorly as outputting 22 V under a non-ideal load, while each battery stack has a lower probability of failure at lower current. Based on the flight requirement of a minimum of 12 hours, we will need at least 4 battery stacks to meet the requirement. With 4 battery stacks, we could have around 15 hours of flight time under nominal load and around 13 hours of flight time under worst-case load. With more battery stacks, we could have more margin for the flight time, but this would also add more weight to the payload, which would affect the flight altitude and also create mechanical stress on the gondola. A balance between flight duration and total mass must therefore be achieved to ensure a successful flight.
