\chapter{GAPS Analysis and Result}
\label{chap:GAPSresult}
This chapter presents the analysis and results from the \ac{GAPS} experiment. We first present the performance of the \ac{GAPS} functional prototype (GFP) in Section.~\ref{chap:GAPSresult:GFP}, including the detector performance validation, thermal performance evaluation, and \ac{TOF} performance evaluation. Then we discuss the ground testing of the full \ac{GAPS} payload in Section.~\ref{chap:GAPSresult:flight}, including the detector performance and thermal performance with the ground cooling system (GCS). Section~\ref{chap:GAPSresult:simulation} presents the atmospheric unfolding simulation. Finally, we summarize the first \ac{GAPS} science flight, which was launched on December 16, 2025 at 5:37 am NZDT from McMurdo Station in Antarctica. Further science results will be presented in future publications as mission data analysis continues.

\section{GAPS functional prototype (GFP) performance}
\label{chap:GAPSresult:GFP}
As described in Section.~\ref{chap:GAPS:GFP}, we set up the \ac{GFP} at Bates during 2021 and 2022. Along with the construction of this prototype ground testing bench, we validated the thermal performance and detector performance simultaneously.

\subsection{Detector Performance and Particle Tracking}
\label{chap:GAPSresult:GFP:detector}

At the beginning of the \ac{GFP}, one concern was that the coupling between the \ac{OHP} tubes and the \ac{Si(Li)} modules would introduce noise to the \ac{Si(Li)} tracker. Prior characterization of individual \ac{GAPS} detectors demonstrated excellent energy resolution \cite{KOZAI2019162695,KOZAI2022166820,PEREZ201812}, but not results obtained in the flight-like environment. 

To disentangle such potential environmental conditions, before integrating the \ac{GFP} tracker, we first installed a single module which is mounted with the discrete preamps with the \ac{OHP} discrete preamps were used for the module calibration as discussed in Ref.~\cite{10168972}. We cooled down the module directly with the cold N$_2$, and calibrated the \ac{Si(Li)} detectors with an Am-241 radioactive source. 

We then compared the X-ray resolutions obtained from the \ac{GFP} configuration and from a standard calibration facility as described in Ref.~\cite{10168972}. Fig.~\ref{fig:GFP_xray} shows the X-ray resolution as a function of peaking time at $-45^\circ$C from the \ac{GFP} and $-37^\circ$C from the standard calibration. The energy resolution with the optimal peaking time ($\sim 4$ $\mu$s) at the \ac{GFP} configuration is below 3 keV. In addition, the energy resolutions with longer peaking times are expected to be better at a lower temperature due to a smaller leak current. By fitting the noise mode, we obtained consistent noise parameters at the \ac{GFP}, with only a smaller leakage current, which scales with temperature These results validate that the \ac{Si(Li)} modules perform well at the system level.

Furthermore, we compared the X-ray resolution when the \ac{OHP} was running or turned off. The red histogram in Fig.~\ref{fig:GFP_xray} shows the X-ray spectrum of one \ac{Si(Li)} strip at $-45^\circ$C obtained when the PID for \ac{OHP} reservoir is off and the shadow shows when it is on. They are very consistent with each other. This test demonstrates that \ac{OHP} operation does not introduce additional noise at the \ac{GFP} configuration, and validates the electrical isolation design.

We studied the \ac{ASIC} electronics using pedestal data from a single \ac{Si(Li)} module. For the \ac{GFP}, modules assembled with the \ac{ASIC} front-end electronics were tested in the laboratory before integration into the system. During this testing, channels with longer circuit traces between the \ac{ASIC} chip and the \ac{Si(Li)} strips exhibited higher noise than the others. This excess noise may have resulted from weaker grounding or greater susceptibility to electromagnetic interference.

To suppress this noise, two additional PCBs were potted onto the FEB using Torr Seal epoxy. These small PCBs were connected to the analog ground through the Al frame, providing closer grounding for channels with longer input traces. This approach was fully validated at the \ac{GFP}.

Fig.~\ref{fig:GAPS_GFP_detector_performance} shows the ENC resolutions of one representative module before (grey) and after (blue) mounting the noise shields from a parallel testing in the lab. In addition, overlaid the plot, the red line shows the ENC resolutions for this module being integrated into the row at the \ac{GFP}. The consistently good ENC resolutions at the \ac{GFP} clearly validate that adopting noise shields is a robust solution to make the ASIC front-end electronics work as well as expected. Due to time constraints, only the 12 \ac{Si(Li)} modules in the top tracker layer were assembled with noise shields, while modules in the middle and bottom layers did not include noise shields during \ac{GFP} operations.

We also performed grounding loop debugging during the \ac{GFP} commissioning. We found that additional noise was introduced when the two rows on the same layer were being operated at the same time. After careful investigation, we identified that the additional noise was caused by the ground loop between the two rows. By design, the rows on the same layer are locked together with metal nut plates in order to maintain the positions of the modules precisely by mechanically constraints. However, the Aluminium frame of \ac{Si(Li)} module also connects with the ground of ASIC electronics. Consequently, a ground loop formed when the adjacent rows were electrically shorted by the metal parts. Fig.~\ref{fig:GFP_GND_loop} demonstrate the electronics connection with and without the issue of ground loop. To confirm the noise source with data, we isolated the two rows on the top layer by disconnecting the nut plates and adding additional nylon spacers between them, and then took the data when six rows in the tracker are powered on. The bottom plot in Fig.~\ref{fig:GFP_GND_loop} compares the pedestal spectrum before and after the row isolation. The ENC resolutions are significantly improved when the adjacent rows on the same layer were electrically isolated.

The above noise debugging and reduction for the \ac{Si(Li)} tracker at the \ac{GFP} testing provided an important opportunity for us to identify and address the issues arising from the interface between the subsystems at the system level. The solution to suppress the noise with additional noise shields has been fully validated by the \ac{GFP} testing and adopted for the \ac{GAPS} flight. A batch of noise shields with a dedicated design, better fitted to the module geometry, was fabricated after the \ac{GFP}. All modules used for the flight were assembled with the noise shields prior to integration. To address the ground loop issue, we improved the mechanical design as well as the row assembly procedure for the flight instrument to secure the isolation but keep the required thermal conductance: the nut plates used to lock the adjacent rows are anodized; a thin (3 mils thick) Kapton tape was placed on both sides of the row; we checked the resistance between adjacent rows with a multimeter after a new row was integrated into the tracker. All of these have greatly mitigated the risks for the \ac{GAPS} flight.

As for the \ac{GFP} scientific runs, the \ac{TOF} provided a master trigger to the \ac{Si(Li)} tracker. After receiving the master trigger, combined with a veto of the busy signal from \ac{Si(Li)} back-ends, the \ac{Si(Li)} DAQ started acquiring the data from the ASICs. It was critical to address the time difference between the trigger generation from \ac{TOF} and trigger reception by \ac{Si(Li)} back-end. With an implementation of a proper time delay, the \ac{Si(Li)} back-end DAQ successfully sampled the muon hits that were triggered by the \ac{TOF} at the \ac{GFP}. The blue histograms in Fig.~\ref{fig:GFP_MIP} show the spectra of minimum ionizing particles (MIP) from muons in the unit of ADC, where each histogram is for the readout from one \ac{Si(Li)}, i.e. the sum of 32 ASIC channels. The \ac{MIP} peaks clearly appear onthe spectra as expected.

In addition to the master trigger from the \ac{TOF}, the FPGA implemented in the \ac{Si(Li)} back-ends provided a Zero Length Encoding (ZLE) algorithm for each module for the data record, in order to reduce the overall data size as well as to suppress accidental background. 

In this data acquisition mode, only the hits which were associated with the \ac{TOF} trigger and over the threshold for zero suppression were recorded by the DAQ. The others, in particular the low-energy hits from noise but were accidentally coincident with the trigger time window, were suppressed to zero. 

In the \ac{GFP} testing, a global zero suppression threshold was set for all modules. The threshold was estimated as $\sim$300 keV which is safely far off the MIP energy range. The cutoff on the MIP spectrum in blue in Fig.~\ref{fig:GFP_MIP} shows the effect introduced by the threshold zero-suppression. These cutoffs vary from module to module, but all are safely far away from the MIP peaks. In addition, no events were observed in the low energy region on the MIP spectrum, which validates that the zero-suppression algorithm works well to suppress the accidental backgrounds.

Due to the ineluctable electronics fluctuations, it is impossible to make all ASIC readout channels identical with each other. So different ASIC readout channels are expected with different responses to the same energy deposition in the \ac{Si(Li)} strips, as indicated by the various locations in ADC from different modules in Fig. 14. In order to perform the energy calibration, we developed an approach with the so-called transfer function to convert the readout ADC to the deposited energy. In this calibration procedure, with the calibration system mounted on the ASIC FEB, we read out the ASIC outputs with various amounts of injected current and built a relation between the input charge (mV) and ASIC readout (ADC) for all channels, which is defined as the transfer function. Based on the simulations, we extracted the charge yield rate with a given energy deposition in the \ac{Si(Li)} strip. By combining the transfer function (ADC to mV) and the charge yield rate (mV to keV/MeV), the \ac{MIP} spectrum was reconstructed. The histograms in red of Fig.~\ref{fig:GFP_MIP} show the MIP energy spectra in MeV and each spectrum is for one \ac{Si(Li)} module. With energy reconstruction applied, all modules at the \ac{GFP} exhibited consistent \ac{MIP} energy deposition, as expected. This method is also applied for all \ac{GAPS} flight detectors.

To validate the electronics and reconstruction chain, we incorporated the \ac{TOF} system and recorded cosmic-muon events. The \ac{TOF} and tracker readout computers were synchronized via the Network Time Protocol (NTP). When the \ac{TOF} issued a master trigger, an event generator sent the same event ID to both systems. Each recorded event also carried a timestamp for offline analysis. The software then merged the separately recorded \ac{TOF} and tracker events using the event ID and timestamp.

After assembly of the \ac{GFP}, I participated in several cosmic-muon runs at Bates. We successfully merged the \ac{TOF} and tracker data and reconstructed muon tracks at the system level. I also developed the Geant4 simulation geometry for the \ac{GFP} setup, enabling us to check the reconstructed muon tracks with the simulation truth information, see Fig.~\ref{fig:GFP_simulation}. With our track reconstruction algorithm, we have successfully demonstrated our capability of capturing cosmic muons. Fig.~\ref{fig:GFP_nice_event} shows an example of the reconstructed muon track. With the merged muon events, the \ac{GAPS} functional prototype has been demonstrated to work well to reconstruct the track at the system level, which includes synchronization of two DAQ systems, the waveform analysis on \ac{TOF} data, hit finding and alignment in the \ac{Si(Li)} tracker, and the event merge. The \ac{OHP} cooling approach developed for \ac{GAPS} has been well demonstrated and published \cite{FUKE2023168102, OKAZAKI201820, doi:10.1142/S2251171714400042}. However, the \ac{GFP} provides an ideal setup to validate the performance of this novel cooling system, as the heat generated from the \ac{Si(Li)} electronics, the thermal coupler between the \ac{OHP} tubes and \ac{Si(Li)} modules, and the surrounding insulating foam are all identical between the \ac{GFP} and flight. The \ac{GFP} testing confirms that the module temperature is strongly driven by the coolant temperature which is well maintained by controlling the temperature of reservoir and heat exchanger. Fig.~\ref{fig:GFP_thermal_result} shows the temperature evolution of the reservoir and three module frames during a representative \ac{GFP} run. All \ac{Si(Li)} modules at \ac{GFP} are successfully cooled down below $-40^\circ$C with the \ac{OHP} running for $\sim$6 hours. The \ac{OHP} performance at \ac{GFP} was consistent with the expectation, further validating the system.

One drawback of the on-ground \ac{GFP} thermal test is the additional convective heat leak relative to flight conditions. As a result, the \ac{GFP} measurements provide a conservative validation of \ac{OHP} performance, and the \ac{GAPS} thermal system is expected to perform better in flight. So, this fact makes a conservative validation of \ac{OHP} performance from the \ac{GFP} testing results, and the \ac{GAPS} thermal system is anticipated to perform even better during the flight. To gain a better understanding of this difference, we evaluated our thermal model based on \ac{GFP} thermal data. The chiller outlet temperature was set at $-70^\circ$C, and a scaled heat load of $Q_0 = 110 \, \text{W}$ was applied to the cold plate. Table.~\ref{tab:GFP_thermal_result} summarizes the results. The average temperature of the cold plate was $-61.0^\circ$C. The insulator outer surface was $26.4^\circ$C on average, consistent with the ambient room temperature. The total outer surface area of the insulator was $3.06 \, \text{m}^2$. From Eq.~\ref{equ:GCS_heatload}, the heat input $Q_2$ was calculated to be $74.9 \pm 1.7 \, \text{W}$. The error was estimated from the temperature measurement uncertainty.

Here, we cross-check the heat input by using the temperatures of the methanol flowing in the tube. Assuming the flow is in quasi-equilibrium and is fully developed, the pressure loss of methanol flowing in the tube can be expressed by the Darcy-Weisbach equation as follows:

\begin{equation}\label{equ:DWpressureloss}
    \Delta P = \lambda \frac{L}{D} \frac{\rho u^2}{2}
\end{equation}

Here, the coefficient of friction in the pipe, $\lambda$, is classified according to Reynolds number, or whether the flow regime is laminar or turbulent, by the following definitions:

\begin{align}\label{equ:friction_coefficient}
\lambda &= 
\begin{cases}
64 \, \text{Re}^{-1}, & \text{Re} \leq 2300, \text{laminar flow}, \\
0.3164 \, \text{Re}^{-0.25}, & \text{Re} > 2300, \text{turbulent flow},
\end{cases} \\
\text{Re} &= \frac{u \, D}{\nu}.
\end{align}

The amount of heat that methanol removes during its flow between the inlet and outlet of the cold plate is expressed by:
\begin{equation}\label{equ:heat_removal}
Q_2 = \Delta T \, \rho \, C_V \, \dot{V}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{equ:volume_flow_rate}
\dot{V} = \pi \, (D/2)^2 \, u = \dot{m}/\rho
\end{equation}

where $\dot{V}$ is the volume flow rate. The pressure loss of methanol between outlet and inlet of the chiller, $\Delta P$, is fixed to $0.3\ MPa$, as defined by the discharge pressure of the chiller pump. For the coolant properties, we referred to \cite{Lemmon2013NISTSR}.

The mass flow rate, $\dot{m}$, which is the same for every tube section, is calculated to be 0.042 kg/s. The calculated values of $Re$ are below 2000 throughout the chiller tube in this case. By combining the above equations and substituting the measured temperature difference $\Delta T$ between inlet and outlet of the cold plate, Eq.~\ref{equ:heat_removal} gives the amount of heat removed from the cold plate by the coolant as $Q_2 \approx 183.8 \pm 5.0 \, \text{W}$. This is consistent with $Q_0 + Q_1 \approx 184.9 \, \text{W}$, which is the amount of heat to be removed from the cold plate.

We successfully demonstrated that a scaled-down \ac{GFP} radiator surrounded by 0.1 m thick insulation could be cooled to $-60^\circ \text{C}$ in the laboratory. We also confirmed that the heat input to the cold plate was balanced by the heat removed through the coolant, and that heat leak could be reduced to a negligible level by filling gaps between insulation sections, see Table.~\ref{tab:GFP_thermal_result} and Fig.~\ref{fig:GFP_thermal_result}.

\section{Atmospheric Unfolding}
\label{chap:GAPSresult:simulation}
For balloon-borne cosmic antinuclei experiments, it is conventional to project the measured flux at the balloon location back to the top of the atmosphere (\ac{TOA}) for comparison with theoretical models. To study this unfolding, I performed a Geant4 simulation of antiprotons propagating through the atmosphere between the \ac{TOA} and \ac{TOI}. Because this propagation is independent of detector type and geometry, the simulation is more generally applicable to balloon-borne experiments.

For this simulation, antiprotons were used as a representative particle species. I generated antiprotons at \ac{TOA} with various energies and isotropic angular distribution. The selected altitude corresponds to the GAPS mission float altitude, $37\ km$. The energy loss and interaction information were recorded for each particle during the propagation through the atmosphere.

The left panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:atmospheric_unfolding} shows the survival probability of antiprotons from \ac{TOA} to \ac{TOI} at 37 km as a function of kinetic energy. As expected, the survival probability increases with energy because higher-energy antiprotons are less likely to be captured by atmospheric nuclei. 

The right panel shows the survival probability binned in different zenith angle. As the zenith angle increases, the path length through the atmosphere increases as well, leading to a lower survival probability. 

Using this result together with the \ac{GAPS} science results, we can unfold the antiproton flux measured at \ac{TOI} back to \ac{TOA} for further scientific interpretation \cite{ROGERS2023102791}. \section{GAPS science flight}

\ac{GAPS} had a successful flight campaign at McMurdo Station, Antarctica during the 2025-2026 austral summer. The payload was launched on Dec 16th, 2025 and floated for 26 days before landing on Jan 10, 2026.
